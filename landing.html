🔬 Architecture

How SilverBot was built

Unlike gold, silver has a genuine dual nature — roughly half industrial demand, half monetary/safe-haven demand. SilverBot was designed from the ground up to surface this, rather than force silver into gold's single-toggle framework.

1
2 Parallel LangGraph Subgraphs
An Industrial subgraph and a Monetary subgraph fetch their respective factors simultaneously before feeding into the scoring node.
2
Dual-Score Calculation
Industrial and Monetary strength are calculated independently against their own weight subtotals — they can diverge, which is the whole point.
3
Dominant Regime Detection
Python compares the two scores to flag ALIGNED or DIVERGENT regimes — divergence is the most academically interesting case.
4
RAG-Grounded Scoring
A FAISS vector store retrieves each factor's historical causal relationship to silver specifically, not just borrowed from gold.
Why Gold's Framework Doesn't Fit Silver
Silver's dual nature breaks single-toggle safe-haven models.
⚠️
Conflicting Signals Get Hidden
A single ON/OFF toggle can average away the most interesting case: industrial-bearish while monetary-bullish (or vice versa) at the same time.
⚠️
No Industrial Demand Signal
Gold has essentially zero industrial use. Silver is ~50% industrial — solar, electronics, EVs — requiring genuinely different factors.
⚠️
Different Supply Dynamics
Silver is largely a byproduct of base-metal mining, unlike gold's primary-mined supply — a structurally different market.

SilverBot: A Dual-Regime Analyzer for Silver's Industrial-Monetary Duality — Cross-Regime Evidence for a Crisis-Type-Dependent Reversal

Raj Tejpal Khatik · MSc Applied AI, University of Warwick
Live preprint on SSRN · Currently under review at a peer-reviewed journal
📄 Read Live on SSRN ↗ Live Demo ↗
Abstract
Silver occupies a theoretically distinct position among precious metals: unlike gold, whose volatility is well-established to be explained primarily by monetary variables, silver's price behavior is simultaneously driven by industrial demand and monetary/safe-haven sentiment, with academic literature explicitly noting that the monetary-variable explanation that works for gold does not hold for silver (Batten, Ciner, & Lucey, 2010). We present SilverBot, a nine-factor, LLM-orchestrated system that decomposes silver's behavior into two parallel sub-scores — Industrial Strength% and Monetary Strength% — alongside a combined classification and an ALIGNED/DIVERGENT regime indicator.
We backtest SilverBot across four independently constructed samples spanning four structurally distinct macro-financial regimes: a stratified sample of 60 dates during a 2025-26 gold-silver uptrend (Backtest 1), 10 genuine macro/geopolitical events within the same window (Backtest 2), 20 stratified dates during the 2020 COVID crash and recovery (Backtest 3), and 20 stratified dates during the 2008 global financial crisis (Backtest 4), using copper as an industrial ground-truth proxy and gold as a monetary ground-truth proxy. In three of four regimes, the Industrial sub-regime outperforms the Monetary sub-regime — significantly so in the largest sample (Backtest 1: 61.7% vs. 40.0%, n=60, McNemar's exact p=0.041).
Critically, this pattern reverses specifically during the 2020 COVID crisis (Monetary 95.0% vs. Industrial 65.0%, n=20), a regime defined by acute monetary/liquidity shock (zero interest rates, negative real yields, VIX above 60) rather than industrial-demand disruption, while the 2008 financial crisis — despite comparable severity — preserves the original Industrial-favoring direction (70.0% vs. 55.0%). We interpret this as evidence that the Industrial-Monetary asymmetry is not a fixed property of silver's dual nature but is itself conditional on the type of crisis — industrial-demand-driven versus monetary-liquidity-driven — a refinement of Batten, Ciner, and Lucey's (2010) classical framework that a single-regime backtest could not have revealed.

1. Introduction and Problem Statement
Silver's behavior does not fit neatly into either the "industrial commodity" or "safe-haven asset" category, and academic literature has emphasized this dual identity explicitly. Batten, Ciner, and Lucey (2010) find that gold's volatility is well-explained by monetary variables, while silver's is not, attributing this to silver's much larger industrial demand share. This finding has been extended to volatility spillovers between the metals (Batten, Ciner, & Lucey, 2015) and echoed in the broader hedge/safe-haven literature (Baur & Lucey, 2010; Baur & McDermott, 2010).
We present SilverBot, a dual-regime LLM-orchestrated system that operationalizes this duality directly, and — following our companion GoldBot study's cross-regime methodology — backtest it across four structurally distinct macro-financial regimes rather than a single window. This design choice proves essential: while the Industrial-Monetary asymmetry predicted by Batten, Ciner, and Lucey holds and is statistically confirmed in our largest sample, it does not hold universally. Testing across a genuine liquidity crisis (2020) reveals a clean reversal, refining rather than merely confirming the classical framework.
2. Related Work
Silver's industrial-monetary duality. Batten, Ciner, and Lucey (2010) is the foundational citation for this paper, finding gold's volatility well-explained by monetary factors while silver's is not.
Precious metals volatility spillovers. Batten, Ciner, and Lucey (2015) find evidence of persistent volatility spillover between silver and gold, motivating our gold-silver ratio and gold-price co-movement as SilverBot's monetary ground-truth proxy.
Gold hedge/safe-haven framework. Baur and Lucey (2010) and Baur and McDermott (2010) establish the weak/strong safe-haven distinction, extended here to silver's dual case using two reference assets (gold, copper).
Crisis heterogeneity in financial markets. While not the primary focus of the precious-metals literature cited above, our finding that asset relationships shift depending on crisis type (liquidity-driven vs. demand-driven) is consistent with a broader recognition in financial economics that "flight to safety" dynamics are not uniform across all crisis episodes — a point this paper's cross-regime design makes directly testable for silver's specific dual-factor structure.
Gap addressed. No existing work, to our knowledge, tests whether the industrial/monetary asymmetry in silver's price behavior is itself conditional on crisis type, using a live, decomposed, LLM-scored classification system across multiple structurally distinct historical regimes.

3.2 Scoring and dual aggregation
Each factor is scored by an LLM (GPT-4o-mini, temperature=0) on a bounded -10 to +10 scale. Combined, Industrial, and Monetary Strength% scores are computed, with an ALIGNED/DIVERGENT classification when the sub-regimes disagree.
4. System Architecture
SilverBot is implemented as a LangGraph state machine with two parallel fetch subgraphs (Industrial, Monetary). Industrial factors and geopolitical risk are retrieved via live Tavily search; industrial production via FRED; gold-silver ratio, risk sentiment, and USD index via yfinance; rate environment and inflation expectations via FRED.
5. Backtesting
5.1 Methodology
Four backtests, each independently constructed:
BacktestDesignnWindowRegime type
1Stratified random dates60Sept 2025–June 2026Uptrend
2Genuine macro/geopolitical events10Sept 2025–June 2026Uptrend, mixed
3Stratified random dates20Jan–June 2020COVID liquidity crisis
4Stratified random dates20Aug 2008–March 2009Global financial/credit crisis
Ground truth: Combined status against silver's own MA50; Monetary regime against gold's MA50 co-movement; Industrial regime against copper's MA50 co-movement. Backtests 3 and 4 use forced-neutral Tier 2 factors throughout (no historical text recovery attempted). Statistical treatment: McNemar's exact test for all paired comparisons, given both compared quantities are scored on identical dates.
5.2 Backtest 1 (n=60): the confirmed baseline finding
MetricResult
Combined accuracy46.7% (28/60)
Naive always-ON baseline66.7% (40/60)
Combined vs. Naive (McNemar)p=0.126
Industrial accuracy61.7% (37/60)
Monetary accuracy40.0% (24/60)
Industrial vs. Monetary (paired McNemar)p=0.041 — significant
This sample was constructed incrementally (initial n=20, plus two independent n=20 expansion batches), with the Industrial-Monetary asymmetry strengthening to statistical significance only at the full n=60.
5.3 Backtest 2 (n=10): genuine events, directionally consistent
MetricResult
Combined accuracy50.0%
Industrial accuracy70.0%
Monetary accuracy40.0%
Directionally consistent with Backtest 1. On February 28, 2026 (the Iran war outbreak), SilverBot's Industrial call correctly matched copper's actual bullish direction while its Monetary call missed gold and silver's actual bullish co-movement — a specific instance of the Industrial signal outperforming during a geopolitical, not purely liquidity-driven, shock.
5.4 Backtest 3 (n=20): the COVID reversal
MetricResult
Combined accuracy40.0% (8/20)
Industrial accuracy65.0% (13/20)
Monetary accuracy95.0% (19/20)
Monetary vs. Industrial (paired McNemar)p=0.070
This is a clean reversal of Backtests 1 and 2. Industrial predictions were 19-of-20 constant OFF; Monetary predictions were 18-of-20 constant ON. Neither sub-regime discriminated much between individual dates — both again show the output-compression pattern documented in our companion GoldBot study — but in this specific regime, the Monetary sub-regime's constant bias happened to align far more consistently with actual gold/silver co-movement than the Industrial sub-regime's bias aligned with copper co-movement.
Why COVID specifically reverses the pattern: the 2020 crisis was defined overwhelmingly by monetary/liquidity shock — the Federal Reserve cut rates to near-zero, real yields turned negative, and VIX spiked above 60 — exactly the regime the Monetary factors (rate environment, risk sentiment) are designed to detect. Industrial demand for silver and copper both collapsed simultaneously under global lockdowns, plausibly reducing the discriminating value of silver-copper co-movement specifically, since both metals' industrial demand moved in the same direction for the same reason rather than reflecting silver-specific industrial dynamics.
5.5 Backtest 4 (n=20): 2008 restores the original direction
MetricResult
Combined accuracy55.0% (11/20)
Industrial accuracy70.0% (14/20)
Monetary accuracy55.0% (11/20)
Naive always-ON baseline50.0% (10/20)
Industrial vs. Monetary (paired McNemar)p=0.549
Despite comparable severity to the COVID crisis, 2008 restores the original Industrial-favoring direction (though not at statistical significance at this sample size). Industrial predictions were fully constant OFF (20/20); Monetary predictions varied more (9 ON, 11 OFF). This is consistent with 2008 being simultaneously a credit crisis and a demand-collapse crisis, rather than the more purely monetary/liquidity-driven character of the COVID shock — silver-copper industrial co-movement plausibly retained more discriminating signal here than in 2020.
5.6 Cross-regime synthesis: a crisis-type-dependent asymmetry
TestRegime typeIndustrialMonetaryWinner
Backtest 1 (n=60)Uptrend61.7%40.0%Industrial (p=0.041, significant)
Backtest 2 (n=10)Mixed events70.0%40.0%Industrial
Backtest 3 (n=20)Liquidity crisis65.0%95.0%Monetary (reversed)
Backtest 4 (n=20)Credit/demand crisis70.0%55.0%Industrial
Three of four regimes favor Industrial, one — the purest liquidity crisis in the sample — favors Monetary decisively. We interpret this not as a failure of the Industrial-Monetary asymmetry, but as a refinement: the asymmetry favoring Industrial signals appears to hold except when the crisis itself is primarily monetary/liquidity in character, in which case the Monetary sub-regime's factors (built specifically around rate environment and risk sentiment) dominate as one would theoretically expect. This is a more precise and more defensible claim than either "Industrial always wins" or "the pattern is unstable" — it is conditional, and the condition (crisis type) is independently identifiable.
6. Generalization (Cross-Regime)
Testing across four structurally distinct regimes reveals that SilverBot's architectural generalization (the pipeline runs correctly and produces classifications regardless of historical period) does not imply generalization of which sub-regime dominates. This distinction — mirroring our companion GoldBot study's separation between mechanical and predictive generalization — is itself a useful methodological contribution: aggregate accuracy figures computed from a single regime cannot reveal crisis-type-conditional reversals of this kind.
7. Limitations
1. The COVID reversal (Backtest 3) does not itself reach conventional statistical significance (p=0.070) at n=20 alone, though it is directionally clean and large in magnitude (30 percentage points).

2. The 2008 result (Backtest 4) is directionally consistent with the original pattern but not statistically significant (p=0.549) at this sample size.

3. Neither Industrial nor Monetary sub-regime shows genuine per-date discrimination in Backtests 3 or 4 — both again exhibit the output-compression pattern (near-constant classifications) documented for GoldBot, meaning the crisis-type-dependent reversal reflects which constant bias happened to align with the regime's trend, not evidence of the model discriminating between distinct events within either crisis.

4. No multiple-comparisons correction was applied across the significance tests reported in this paper.

5. Tier 2 (Tavily-sourced) weight remains 32/100 forced-neutral across all four backtests; GDELT-based historical recovery was not extended to SilverBot's three Tier 2 factors in any backtest.

6. No validation against an established academic geopolitical risk benchmark (e.g., the Caldara-Iacoviello GPR index) has been performed for SilverBot.

7. Only one liquidity-crisis regime (2020) and one credit/demand-crisis regime (2008) were tested; replication across additional instances of each crisis type would strengthen the crisis-type-dependent thesis considerably.

8. The initial ground-truth implementation error (checking sub-regimes against silver's own direction rather than the gold/copper proxies) was caught and corrected before final results were computed in all four backtests.
8. Conclusion and Future Work
We present SilverBot, a dual-regime LLM-orchestrated system decomposing silver's classically-documented industrial/monetary duality (Batten, Ciner, & Lucey, 2010), and subject it to backtesting across four structurally distinct macro-financial regimes — a stratified 2025-26 uptrend sample (n=60, statistically confirming Industrial > Monetary at p=0.041), a genuine-event sample from the same window, the 2020 COVID liquidity crisis, and the 2008 global financial crisis. Our central finding is that the Industrial-Monetary asymmetry is not a fixed property of silver's dual nature: it holds in three of four regimes but reverses cleanly and substantially during the COVID crisis specifically, a regime distinguished from 2008 by its overwhelmingly monetary/liquidity-driven (rather than credit- and demand-driven) character. This crisis-type-dependent reversal is, to our knowledge, a novel refinement of the classical industrial-monetary framework, made visible only by testing across genuinely distinct crisis types rather than relying on a single backtest window.
Future work should prioritize: (a) testing additional instances of both liquidity-driven crises (e.g., 2022's rapid rate-hiking cycle) and demand-driven crises to test whether the crisis-type-dependent reversal generalizes beyond these two specific historical episodes; (b) extending the GDELT historical-recovery pipeline to SilverBot's Tavily-sourced factors; (c) validating geopolitical_risk scoring against the Caldara-Iacoviello GPR index; (d) investigating whether the output-compression pattern documented across both crisis regimes reflects a general limitation of the LLM scoring architecture under extreme conditions, consistent with findings in our companion GoldBot study.
