# Silver Causal Chain Reference Document

## Rate Environment (Fed Rate + Real Yields)
Silver, like gold, is a non-yielding asset, so rising real yields and Fed rates increase the opportunity cost of holding it, historically pressuring prices lower. However, silver's monetary-driver sensitivity is somewhat weaker than gold's because roughly half its demand is industrial, not investment-driven. During the 2022 Fed hiking cycle, silver fell alongside gold but also decoupled somewhat during periods of strong industrial demand (e.g., solar buildout), showing the dual-nature effect. Scoring thresholds: Real yields below 0% = strongly bullish (+7 to +10); 0-1% = mildly bullish (+2 to +5); 1-2% = neutral (-2 to +2); above 2% = strongly bearish (-7 to -10). Fed Funds Rate above 5% = bearish pressure; below 2% = bullish; cutting cycles are bullish regardless of absolute level.

## Gold-Silver Ratio
The gold-silver ratio (price of gold divided by price of silver) has historically ranged between roughly 15:1 and 100:1, with a long-run average closer to 60-70:1 in the modern era. When the ratio spikes above 80-90, it has historically signaled silver is undervalued relative to gold, often preceding silver outperformance ("catch-up rallies") as the ratio mean-reverts. Conversely, a ratio below 50 suggests silver has already outperformed and may be due for consolidation. Scoring thresholds: ratio above 90 = strongly bullish for silver (+7 to +10, undervalued); 70-90 = mildly bullish (+3 to +6); 50-70 = neutral (-2 to +2); below 50 = bearish (-5 to -8, silver overextended relative to gold).

## Risk Sentiment (VIX + S&P 500)
Silver's safe-haven behavior is less reliable than gold's - during acute market stress, silver sometimes rallies alongside gold (flight to safety) but can also fall if the stress signals an economic slowdown that would hurt industrial demand. This dual reaction makes risk sentiment a genuinely ambiguous signal for silver compared to gold. Scoring thresholds: VIX below 15 = calm markets, mildly bearish for silver as safe-haven demand fades (-2 to -4); VIX 15-25 = neutral to mildly bullish (0 to +3); VIX above 30 = high stress, historically bullish via safe-haven flows (+4 to +7) unless accompanied by clear recession signals, in which case industrial demand fears can offset this. S&P 500 monthly growth above 3% = risk-on, bearish for silver (-3 to -5); decline below -3% = risk-off, bullish (+3 to +5).

## Mining Supply Growth
Unlike gold, which is primarily mined as a target metal, roughly 70% of silver supply comes as a byproduct of mining copper, zinc, lead, and gold. This makes silver supply structurally inelastic - miners cannot easily ramp up silver production in response to price signals alone, since output depends on base-metal mining economics. Supply disruptions (strikes, regulatory changes, ore grade declines) in major byproduct-producing countries like Mexico, Peru, and China have historically caused outsized price reactions relative to gold's more primary-supply-driven market. Scoring thresholds: reported supply growth below 0% (contraction) = bullish (+5 to +8); 0-2% growth = neutral (-1 to +2); above 3% growth = bearish (-4 to -6, oversupply pressure).

## Solar Panel Demand
Photovoltaic (solar panel) manufacturing has become a genuinely new structural demand driver for silver since the 2010s, now accounting for an estimated 15-20% of total global silver demand and growing as global solar capacity expands. This is silver's closest parallel to gold's central bank buying trend - a real, measurable new source of demand with no historical precedent. Strong government green-energy policy commitments (China, EU, US Inflation Reduction Act-style subsidies) have historically correlated with silver demand upticks. Scoring thresholds: reported solar demand growth above 10% YoY = strongly bullish (+6 to +9); 3-10% growth = mildly bullish (+2 to +5); flat or declining solar capacity additions = bearish (-3 to -5).

## USD Index
Silver, like gold, is priced in US dollars globally, so dollar strength mechanically makes silver more expensive for non-dollar buyers, pressuring demand and price. This mechanism is essentially identical to gold's. Scoring thresholds: DXY above 108 = strongly bearish (-6 to -9); 103-108 = mildly bearish (-2 to -5); 98-103 = neutral (-1 to +1); below 98 = bullish (+4 to +7, weak dollar supports silver).

## Industrial Production Index
Since roughly half of silver demand is industrial (electronics, EVs, medical devices, solar), the health of the broader manufacturing economy directly drives a large share of physical silver demand - a mechanism gold simply does not have. Rising industrial production historically correlates with stronger silver demand and price support, independent of monetary conditions. Scoring thresholds: YoY industrial production growth above 3% = bullish (+5 to +8); 0-3% growth = neutral (-1 to +2); contraction (negative YoY) = bearish (-5 to -8, signals weakening industrial demand).

## Geopolitical Risk
Geopolitical conflict affects silver through two channels simultaneously: safe-haven investment demand (similar to gold, though weaker) and supply-chain disruption risk, since major silver mining regions (Latin America, parts of Asia) can be affected by regional instability, sanctions, or trade restrictions. This dual channel means geopolitical risk can sometimes push silver in both directions at once - bullish via safe-haven flows, bearish if it threatens global industrial activity and thus silver's industrial demand base. Scoring thresholds: major active conflict or escalation involving key mining/manufacturing regions = bullish (+5 to +8); moderate tensions with limited economic disruption = mildly bullish (+2 to +4); de-escalation or resolution = bearish (-3 to -5).

## Inflation Expectations
Silver's reputation as an inflation hedge is, like gold's, inconsistent historically - it has protected purchasing power over very long horizons but shown weak correlation with inflation over shorter periods, and its industrial-demand component complicates the pure inflation-hedge narrative further. Scoring thresholds: 5-year breakeven inflation expectations above 3% = bullish (+4 to +7); 2-3% = neutral (-1 to +2); below 2% = bearish (-3 to -5, low inflation expectations reduce hedging demand).
